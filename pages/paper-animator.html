PaperAnimator

Read the paper. Mark it up. That is the presentation.

Open a research paper and get a source-faithful animated presentation you can edit, verify and export. It all runs in the browser: the PDF is read on your own machine, and nothing is uploaded, stored or trained on.

01

What it is

An editor that opens on the real pages of a paper, rendered as printed, with text you drag across as in any PDF reader — and an empty storyboard. Nothing is composed for you, because a talk assembled by a machine is a talk you then argue with. The empty storyboard offers to draft one from the whole paper; it stays a thing you asked for.

Highlight a passage and a bar arrives on it. Click an instrument, or pick one up and drop it on the words:

  • Statement — the passage, set as large as it fits, read aloud as it appears.
  • Big number — the statistic at full size, with the sentence that qualifies it.
  • Pull quote — a quotation, attributed to its page.
  • Build a list — one line per sentence, each arriving as it is spoken, each separately cited.
  • Beat by beat — one scene per sentence: a paragraph becomes a sequence in one gesture.
  • Figure — a crop of the page at full resolution, with its caption.
  • Spotlight — those exact words marked inside the sentence around them.
  • Side by side — this passage against one you kept earlier, in two columns.

02

Why I built it

Health Science Research Methods taught me to read a paper properly, then asked me to present one. Two different skills, and the gap between them is where accuracy dies: somewhere in the retyping a hedge falls off, a confidence interval is left behind, and “was associated with” becomes “caused”.

Every tool that offered to help made it worse, because they all generate. A summariser writes fluent sentences the paper does not contain, and then you are proofreading a machine instead of presenting a study.

I wanted the inverse: a tool structurally incapable of writing a sentence the paper does not say. That constraint is the product.

03

The most interesting interaction

The highlight is the citation. A mark carries the quads of what you marked, down to the character. That is not metadata attached afterwards; it is the scene’s source. There is no later step where a citation gets added, so there is none to skip or get wrong.

It runs both ways, visibly. Passages a scene already uses are washed in mint on the page, with a numbered chip in the margin that jumps to that scene — so the paper itself shows what you have covered. And clicking a claim on the canvas draws a thread back to the words it came from.

While you carry an instrument, the scene it would make floats under the cursor — drawn by the same renderer as the canvas and the export, so the preview cannot promise what the drop would not deliver.

None of it needs the pointer. Click a line to take its sentence, ⌥↑ to widen to its paragraph, then press a letter: S statement, N number, Q quote, B build, X beats, F figure, H spotlight, V voice-over. M opens the animation gallery for whatever you are looking at.

04

The problem being solved

Two guarantees, both enforced by the architecture rather than by discipline.

The composer selects; it never writes. Every on-screen atom and narration cue is either a span of the source or a linking phrase carrying no claim. Comprehension is a ranking problem over the paper’s own sentences, so the result is grounded by construction, not by review. Each atom carries one provenance label: extracted, paraphrase, explanation, connective, authored or unsupported. A paper that never states its limitations gets an empty limitations list, and the interface says so — that absence is information about the paper.

Playback is a pure function of time. One function, resolveFrame(project, tMs), is the only source of animated values, imported by the live canvas, the static previews and the exporter alike; nothing inside the scene surface animates via CSS or a motion library. That turns “preview matches export” from a QA problem into a type signature — and a harness draws both renderings of the same frame side by side to prove it, while asserting the reduced-motion contract: movement off, nothing travels.

The animation catalogue works the same way. Twenty-one entrances and six sustained motions in one file, each a pure function of progress, run by the storyboard tiles, the stage and the exporter alike — so an animation cannot look one way while you choose it and another once chosen.

05

Current capabilities

  • A real reader. Page rasters as printed, a real selectable text layer, and a dock of instruments you drop onto what you marked.
  • Eight instruments, each producing a scene that already carries its source, plus Add to this scene and a voice-over cue.
  • An animation gallery where every tile is your own scene, animating for real — choosing is watching rather than guessing. It leads with the entrances built for what the scene is about, judged by what occupies most of the frame.
  • Three further controls: sustained motion after the entrance lands, choreography that hands out delays so elements arrive in sequence, and a volume that takes the same move louder or quieter without changing its character. Each applies immediately and is one undo away.
  • Project-wide Source Integrity, telling you how much is grounded and which parts need review before you present it.
  • Exports that open in real software. MP4 (H.264 through WebCodecs, each frame carrying its own timestamp so the duration is the real one, falling back to WebM where H.264 cannot be written — and saying so), a ZIP of one PNG per scene, an editable PPTX whose speaker notes carry the narration and its citations, a searchable PDF with an invisible text layer and a source appendix, SRT and VTT tracks, a timed transcript with the page each line came from, and a project file that reopens with its assets.
  • A sample paper generated in the browser and run through the same pipeline your own paper would take — so there is something real to try without uploading anything.
  • Twelve verification harnesses under tools/, each driving a real browser against the production build: the flow, the reader at phone and tablet widths, motion, export parity, every export run for real, playback sampled from the exported video, and a keyboard-only path.
06

Design & technical decisions

Comprehension is selection, not generation. The plan routed comprehension through a hosted model; the implementation ranks the paper’s own sentences in the browser. A component that only chooses existing sentences cannot invent one — and it removes the account, the key and the round trip in the same move.

One source view, not two. The plan had an outline mode beside a pages mode. The outline is gone: a re-typeset paper is not the paper, and two notions of “where you are” made it feel like a tool rather than like reading.

Narration is honest about what it cannot do. It is read by the browser’s speech engine, which cannot be captured into a video file. So the video is silent and carries captions, and the captions, transcript and speaker notes hold every spoken line. The exports table says so, rather than leaving somebody to find out after a render.

Where the build departs from the plan, the build is the record. Ten design documents sit in the repository — data model, extraction pipeline, render engine, design system, editor and export specs, quality gates, roadmap — and the two places the implementation diverged are written up as departures with their reasoning, not quietly edited to match.

07

What changed across iterations

Plan

Ten design documents before a line of code — product definition, non-negotiables, architecture, risks, and a definition of done. Two of its assumptions did not survive contact.

Then

The outline mode was removed. A re-typeset paper is not the paper; the reader now shows real page rasters with a real text layer over them.

Then

The hosted model was removed. Ranking the paper’s own sentences in the browser is both more accurate and less to explain.

Then

One renderer for the canvas, the previews and the export. The determinism contract that made “what you see is what you get” provable rather than aspirational.

Then

The animation gallery became your own scenes. Generic preview tiles replaced with the actual scene, animating, so the choice is made by watching.

Latest

MP4 by default, with WebM as the fallback — H.264 through WebCodecs, and an export that tells you when it had to fall back rather than handing you a file that will not open.

08

What comes next

  • Narration in the video file. The biggest gap. Browser speech cannot be captured, so it needs an on-device synthesiser rendering to a buffer the muxer can take — a problem I have solved once already, in Manifester.
  • Tables as first-class as figures. The extraction pipeline reads them; there is no instrument that turns one into a scene yet.
  • Multi-paper comparison. Side by side currently pairs two passages from the same paper. Journal club wants two papers.
  • A verification pass with people who present research for a living. Source Integrity is my model of grounding, and it deserves testing against somebody else’s.
