Bellwether

The early sign, written down. A minute a day, and the pattern comes out over weeks.

Build a check-in around your own situation — skin, gut, migraine, POTS, fatigue, a diet experiment — answer it in about a minute, and watch the trends come out. No account, no server: once loaded, it makes no network requests at all.

Formerly Health Journal. Same app, same data on your device, a name that says what it is for.

Not medical advice. The app says so too, in its README and inside the product. It surfaces possible patterns in your own logs and never claims a cause.

01

What it is

A private symptom journal that lives on your device. You design the check-in — from a question pack for skin, diet or POTS, or your own questions in your own words — answer it once a day, and it accumulates into a timeline, trends, flares, experiments and a page you can hand to a clinician.

Four things can reach a network, all opt-in, all off until you turn them on, and every one names what it is sending before it sends it: importing your own notes, AI observations with your own key, encrypted sync, and daily weather context.

02

Why I built it

Every health app I tried asked the wrong questions — built around somebody else’s condition, or a subscription, or a server that wanted my symptom history. I wanted a record I own, shaped by what I am tracking, still legible a year later.

The clinical half of my training kept pointing at one moment. A specialist appointment is ten minutes every few months, and it opens with “so how have you been?” — the question memory answers worst, because memory reaches for the last bad week and the last bad week is not the year. The answer was already in the journal, in no form anybody could hand over.

03

The most interesting interaction

Sun & Outdoor Light. One tap starts a session, and what opens is the sun’s own arithmetic, not an illustration of it. The curve is real solar elevation at your latitude today. The shaded band is where there is enough UVB for synthesis to be plausible. The thicker overlay is what you have spent outside. The disc is the sun, now.

So it is flat and bandless in December, because December is — and at high latitude in midwinter it says the sun does not rise, rather than printing a nonsense time.

Beside it: a stopwatch, live UV, the sun’s height and bearing, minutes outside, ambient dose, and an estimate labelled as what it is — “estimated vitamin D: ~1,800–2,600 IU, research-model estimate · not a measurement.” Personalised on the UV over your actual session, latitude, date, skin type, age, exposed area, clothing, sunscreen and shade, and printed as a range, because the honest width is wider than any single number implies.

The model plateaus rather than climbs. Past roughly one minimal erythemal dose your skin stops making more, and a number that keeps rising is quietly telling you a longer burn is a bigger benefit.

Then the seam. Tap any finding — a coincidence on Insights, half an experiment, a lab period, a flare — and those exact days light up across the whole app. History reorders to show them; the temperature trace and the thirty-day sun history mark them. One set of days, one banner naming what lit them, one Clear, surviving navigation. That is what makes it one memory rather than five features.

04

The problem being solved

A sample of one with no control group is easy to lie to yourself with. You start a cream, you have a good fortnight, the cream gets the credit. The hard problem is not collecting the data — it is refusing to over-read it.

So there is one evidence ladder for everything it claims. Useful on an experiment and Useful on an insight are the same claim: four rungs counted in paired days and how far apart those days sit. Forty days from a single fortnight caps at Emerging, however clean it looks. Below the bar nothing is reported — Collecting is a progress bar and a count, not a hedged result, because a sentence with a caveat on it is still a sentence you will remember.

There are no confidence percentages anywhere. A percentage is a promise about a population, and this is a sample of one. Why am I seeing this? opens the working instead: usable observations, days missing one side, the comparison window, the split, consistency, the lag used, and the three limitations true of every finding this app can make.

Experiments split your days at your own median and grade on the smaller half — fifty days with two above the line is two days of evidence in fifty days’ clothing. Under the result, every paired day is a dot placed by its own two values. Two clouds at different heights is the finding; two overlapping clouds is the null result, drawn identically, because the app is not more excited about a positive.

05

Current capabilities

  • A check-in you designed. Question packs to start from, custom questions in your own words, each drawn with the control it will actually use — ten rungs, a yes beside a no, a keypad, three lines of text — and the cost printed above it: 14 questions, about 50 seconds a day.
  • The living timeline. Every day is a card; the day you just filled in flies into place as the first card and the rail draws downward into the days you have not lived yet.
  • Sun sessions with real solar geometry, first outdoor light after waking kept separately as a circadian number, the best upcoming window, and the next vitamin-D-producing one — which correctly says none in the next week in a British January instead of inventing one.
  • Personal experiments from a picker that can put anything against anything: a symptom against the pollen count, a meal against the next morning, a lab value against a season.
  • Labs & measurements that keep the reference range your laboratory printed. A result without one gets no band, no colour and no verdict — this app does not know what normal is for somebody else’s assay. New values arrive with the delta counting up beside them and the gap filled in with what else the journal held.
  • Daily context, opt-in. Temperature, humidity, pressure and its change, UV, daylight, air quality, pollen. Coordinates round to about a kilometre before anything is written or sent — the export column is latitude_coarse because that is all there ever was.
  • Flares you mark yourself. Nothing is detected for you; a run of high scores is not always a flare, and you are the one who knows.
  • The appointment pack. Pick a window — since my last appointment included — and get a page that prints in the order a consultation runs.
  • Encrypted sync, opt-in. Encrypted on your device before it is uploaded, with a passphrase that never leaves it, derived at 600,000 PBKDF2-SHA256 iterations. Local saves never wait for the network.
  • Import your own notes. Paste a notes file, a chat with yourself or three sentences of prose, or hand over a screenshot, and it becomes meals, doses, numbers and notes on the dates your own notes give — with every proposed row shown beside the words it came from before one is written.
  • About you. Sixteen kinds of standing fact across Body, Mind and Life — conditions, allergies, operations, family history, what is weighing on you, how you live — printed at the top of every appointment pack. Nothing to add is a real answer, stored with the day you gave it.
  • Search. One box over every note, meal, dose, flare, lab result and hour outside. It answers on the first keystroke, and itch>=8 -dairy is a question you can actually ask.
  • Get your data out as CSV, multi-sheet Excel or JSON with date-range filters, plus a read-only backup viewer.
06

Design & technical decisions

Built for one thumb. Sheets rise from the bottom edge and stay welded to it, so the action row is the nearest thing to the thumb rather than the furthest. Sized in dvh, because vh on iOS Safari means the viewport without the URL bar — which is how a web form hides its own Save button. They clear the keyboard on both engines, through two mechanisms feeding one CSS variable.

Months as rows, not weeks as columns. A phone gives the card about 330 px: 5 px per day by week, 9 px by day-of-month. Squares that small are not a tap target, so a tap names the day in a readout under the grid, beside the button that opens it — landing on the 14th when you meant the 15th is something you see rather than a screen you back out of.

Colour is never the only channel. The grid is one tab stop with arrow-key movement, every square announces its date and score, and a month-by-month table underneath states the same figures in words for anyone who cannot use colour.

Soft Clinical. Graphite in the dark, warm off-white in the light, muted blue with sage, lavender and clay, and a hint of neobrutalism — borders a notch above a hairline, hard offset shadows, buttons that press into them. Backdrops are CSS: no WebGL, no canvas, no device test, nothing to fail.

The name is asked for, and refusable. A name and an age are a doorway, not step one of a registration, and the screen argues by consequence rather than nagging: the greeting quotes your name back as you type, and an appointment pack header fills in beside it. Age is stored as the year you were born, so it is still right in three years.

07

What changed across iterations

Thirty-nine releases, from the project’s changelog. One direction: less to answer, more to trust.

1.15

Insights rebuilt around the questions people actually ask — the spread of days, flares, and the long view, instead of nine charts in a row.

1.16

The appointment pack. The realisation that a file for a spreadsheet and a page for a person are not the same errand, and the second one is the one that changes an appointment.

1.17

One tap is a whole day. The Daily Pulse; detail moved behind the rating rather than in front of it; navigation cut to two destinations and one verb.

1.18

How it’s drawn is your choice, and every choice says what it costs. Also: sheets stopped moving the page underneath them.

1.19

Quick Add is shaped like your condition. The dashboard assembles itself out of your own setup answers rather than filing them away to be discovered later.

1.20

The first run knows who it is for — and shows you the survey you are signing up for, printed exactly as it will be answered tomorrow morning.

1.21

Four things a journal cannot get from asking questions — weather context, sun sessions, personal experiments and lab results — and the one seam that makes them a single product rather than four features: tap a finding, and those exact days light up everywhere.

1.23

One hand. Every sheet welded to the bottom edge, every action within reach of the thumb holding the phone.

1.29

A new name: Bellwether. The name, the mark, and everything that carried the old one — for a journal whose whole job is the early sign.

1.33

One box that finds anything, and an import that reads a paragraph. Search across the whole journal, and months of shorthand turned into rows you approve one by one.

1.35

Thirteen screens of saying no, answered once. The day closes, and the stack of days behind it is visible.

1.38

Your calendar, opt-in. The only record most people keep of what they agreed to, read beside the days it shaped.

1.39

The three questions an appointment opens with. The standing record: who you are, not just how you have been, printed above all the arithmetic.

08

What comes next

  • More of the ladder, applied harder. It governs experiments and insights; the weather-context observations should be held to the same bar in the same module.
  • The iOS app to parity. The native build and Home Screen widget exist; the widget currently shows less than the dashboard knows.
  • Wearable import, widened. Google Fit and Fitbit takeout archives import today; sleep staging and resting heart rate should be first-class metrics rather than numbers you typed.
  • A second opinion on the vitamin D model. A research-model estimate, labelled as one everywhere it appears, and due a review against the literature by somebody who is not me.
