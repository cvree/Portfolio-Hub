Comp Tracker

A broadcast is a video of a match, not a record of one.

Paste a link to an Overwatch Champions Series VOD and get back an auditable hero-composition timeline: automatic HUD calibration, live-versus-replay frame classification, template-matched hero detection with a temporal swap model, a staged SQLite database, and a static fan-facing site that renders the result with click-through evidence. Everything downstream of the video is plain Python and OpenCV; the site is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript. No frameworks, no server, no paid services.

01

What it is

One product with one workflow, and every page belongs to a step of it: submit a game → automatic processing → review what was detected → approve or correct → published match stats. The navigation has five entries, in that order, and everything else is reached from the screen it belongs to.

The pipeline has processed one complete real map end to end, and the page that publishes it says so precisely: Al Qadsiah versus Twisted Minds, OWCS 2026 NA/EMEA Stage 2 Playoffs Day 2, Nepal — 3 control rounds, 299 frames sampled (196 hero-readable, 103 skipped with reasons), 1,950 accepted slot reads, two temporally confirmed hero swaps with before-and-after evidence crops, and seven suspected swaps rejected as noise.

02

Why I built it

I play and lead competitively, and the single most useful thing after a match is knowing what the other team actually ran and when they changed it. That information exists — it is on screen for the whole broadcast — and it exists nowhere afterwards. A VOD is a video of a match, not a record of one. Every serious answer requires somebody to scrub the footage by hand.

The version of that problem I found interesting was not “can a model read a HUD”. It was: how do you build a system whose output somebody should be willing to trust? A composition tracker that is right most of the time is worse than useless — it is a source people will cite. So the real design problem was evidence: making every published number traceable to the frame it was read from, and making the system refuse to publish anything it cannot show its working for.

03

The most interesting interaction

The swap review. A hero swap is the highest-value fact the pipeline can produce and the easiest one to get wrong: a dead portrait dims and looks like a different hero, a killcam cuts to another player’s view, an ultimate changes the art. A naive detector produces a stream of swaps that did not happen.

So the temporal model treats a change as a hypothesis, and the review screen shows you the hypothesis and its verdict together — including the ones it threw out. Each row states the slot, the timestamp, and the reasoning in plain language: “detector said rejected (candidate sojourn seen 2× then sym returned — noise, not a swap)”, or “detector said confirmed (lucio persisted 3 obs while juno no longer detected)”.

Showing the rejections is the part that matters. A system that only displays what it accepted is asking to be trusted. A system that shows you the seven it discarded, and why, is showing you its judgement — and lets you catch the one it got wrong.

The whole screen is keyboard-driven, because reviewing evidence is repetitive work: j/k move, a approves, c corrects, f flags, and ⇧A approves every clean read at once — where “clean” is defined on screen as the detector agreeing with itself across the whole stint at 90% or better, the same threshold the pipeline uses before it will publish anything without asking.

04

The problem being solved

Reading a hero portrait at 35×35 pixels out of a compressed broadcast stream is not one problem. It is five, and the pipeline is arranged as five answers.

  • Where is the HUD? Calibration finds the ten portrait boxes computationally — chip rows in HSV, a RANSAC grid fit, then pixel-evidence verification — and writes a reusable, resolution-independent layout profile. Below a confidence of 0.55 it refuses, with reasons, rather than producing a layout it cannot stand behind.
  • Is this frame even gameplay? A structural filter separates live play from replays, scoreboards, the analyst desk and transitions, so a replay can never create a composition.
  • What hero is that? A ranked template match returns the candidate, the runner-up and the margin between them — and returns UNKNOWN rather than guessing when the margin is too thin.
  • Did it change, or did the picture change? Temporal consensus with hysteresis: a swap needs persistence and displacement, not a single disagreeing frame.
  • Can a template be trusted at all? Templates are cut only from a build slice, separated from a holdout by a dead zone, gated on size, sharpness, contrast, flatness, overlay bands and confusion with another hero — then validated on frames they have never seen.

And the limits are published, not buried. The README has a section titled “What is NOT production-ready”, and it is the most useful thing in the repository. Hero coverage is 8 of 52 on the best template package — those eight fully provenanced and validated across 1,861 held-out trials with zero confident wrong answers, and the other 44 heroes have simply never appeared in a broadcast this pipeline has processed, so no amount of reprocessing covers them. Two heroes are named as genuinely confusable at that resolution in grayscale, with the measured failure rate stated rather than averaged into a pass. A colour-based veto was built to fix it, measured against real crops, and removed, because it blocked none of the actual leaks while vetoing legitimate reads.

05

Current capabilities

  • One link in. Submit has one required field. The link is classified as you type, offline; known broadcasts autofill the rest; advanced options stay shut; there is exactly one final button.
  • The site fills itself. A scheduled scan reads every verified official channel and produces a discovery layer: every found broadcast with the event, stage, day and region its own title states — and null where it does not — joined against the official calendar and against the published record, with the one action that moves it forward.
  • Three datasets, never merged. The published record (approved, evidence-backed, safe to state as fact), the working record (runs, sources, what is happening), and the discovery record (what the scan found, never a fact a person confirmed). An empty export renders an honest empty state, never invented games.
  • Finding is not detection. Nothing in the discovery layer can publish a composition, approve a source or touch the database. A discovered row carries the video’s own metadata and a reading of its title — never a score, a composition or a result.
  • Full stats over approved data only — heroes, compositions, maps, teams and swaps as five tabs, with nested team and hero profiles reached from the table you were reading.
  • Site search on every page behind a button, / and ⌘K, indexing games, teams, heroes, maps and the product’s own pages straight from the export, with no service and no index file to rebuild. Fuzzy, because “qadsia” and “midseson” are what people actually type.
  • An in-browser calibration wizard — the one operator tool that works fully on the published copy.
  • A Windows application build, and a reproducibility gate that checks template directories, marker assets, the database milestone, evidence paths and page wiring before anything ships.
  • 107 Python test modules, all runnable offline with no network and no VOD.
06

Design & technical decisions

Detector consensus is never presented as human labelling. Every crop records its labelSource. Labels produced by temporal stint consensus are strong — hundreds of agreeing frames decide each one, so no single template can influence the crop it is tested on — but they cannot catch a systematically mislabelled set, and the distinction is preserved all the way through the system rather than collapsed at the first convenient point.

Readiness is never one number. Hero coverage is reported per package as four separate things — covered, sound, traceable, proven — with the blocker and the next action for each hero, because a single percentage would let a package look ready by averaging over the heroes nobody has evidence for.

The template describes the hero, not the player. A portrait region finder locates the sub-rectangle of a calibrated slot from the footage itself, so a template does not accidentally encode whoever happened to be on that hero when it was cut.

Scheduled jobs are read-only by construction. The two workflows that run on a schedule sync a registry and discover broadcasts. Neither downloads a video, writes a composition or approves a source. Everything that mutates the committed milestone is manual dispatch only, so an unattended job cannot race CI or change a published fact.

The hero art crops itself. Official splash art is a scene — the hero stands off-centre in a blurred location with a flat band at the bottom for page text — so cropping down the middle gave portraits that were mostly backdrop with the head clipped off. The cropper now finds the hero first: the flat band has no detail, the hero is the only thing in focus, so a block-wise sharpness map isolates the figure and the heaviest run of columns separates them from their props. There is no per-hero table; a hero added tomorrow frames itself.

The language-model advisor contributes nothing to any published number. It offers closed-vocabulary suggestions behind a human gate and wording help on calibration failures. Its live suggestion quality has not been measured against a labelled set, and until it has, the documentation says to treat a suggestion as a prompt to go look, not as evidence.

07

What changed across iterations

First

Frame grabs and a hand-tuned crop box. It read one broadcast at one resolution and broke on the next one.

Then

Computational calibration that finds the portrait boxes itself and refuses below a confidence threshold — the change that made a second source possible at all.

Then

UNKNOWN as a first-class answer. The detector stopped picking a best guess and started reporting the margin, which is what made the confidence numbers mean anything.

Then

Held-out validation and the leave-one-hero-out test. Removing a hero from a package and checking that its portraits read UNKNOWN rather than as somebody else — the test that found the juno/kiriko confusion.

Then

The review gate. Four separate lists — matches, runs, sources and tournaments — collapsed into one games list and one review screen, because they were four views of the same thing.

Then

The site fills itself. The finder had always scanned official channels, but its snapshot was only rendered on the operator’s own machine — so the live site showed whatever a human last pushed while dozens of real broadcasts sat in a committed file.

Latest

Hero art that shows the hero, and the surface overhaul around it — portraits that are readable at the 28–40 px the comp strips actually render them at.

08

What comes next

  • A second broadcast. Almost every limit in this project resolves the same way: the thresholds are fitted to one source, the confusable pair needs a better template from different footage, and 44 heroes need footage that contains them. One more processed map is worth more than any amount of tuning.
  • Series-level structure. Only one map of one match is ingested; series scores are not recorded and the map winner is operator-supplied rather than read.
  • Round detail read rather than told. Round boundaries currently come from clustering the centre point-emblem at the sample rate — good to about one sample — and per-round control percentages are not read at all, which the map page states as an honest fallback rather than filling in.
