Live Health tooling v1.21 Local-first PWA

Health Journal

A minute a day, and the pattern comes out over weeks.

You build a check-in around your own situation — skin, gut, migraine, POTS, fatigue, a diet experiment — answer it in about a minute, and watch the trends come out. There is no account and no server. Out of the box, once it has loaded, it makes no network requests at all and works completely offline.

Open the live app → Source on GitHub →

Not medical advice. The app says this too, at the top of its own README and inside the product. It is a personal tracking tool; it surfaces possible patterns in your own logs and never claims a cause.

01

What it is

A daily health journal that lives on your device. You design the check-in — starting from a question pack for skin, diet, POTS or whatever you are tracking, or writing your own questions in your own words — answer it once a day, and it accumulates into a timeline, trends, flares, experiments and, when you need it, a page you can hand to a clinician.

Three things can reach a network, all opt-in, all off until you turn them on, and all leaving everything else working exactly as it did: AI observations with your own key, encrypted cross-device sync, and daily weather context.

02

Why I built it

Every health app I tried asked the wrong questions. They were built around a condition somebody else had, or around a subscription, or around a server that wanted my symptom history. What I wanted was simpler and harder: a record I own, shaped by what I am actually tracking, that is still legible a year later.

The clinical half of my training kept pointing at the same moment. A specialist appointment is ten minutes every few months and it opens with “so how have you been?” — the question human memory answers worst, because memory reaches for the last bad week and the last bad week is not the year. Everything needed to answer it properly was already in the journal and none of it was in a form anybody could hand over.

03

The most interesting interaction

Sun & Outdoor Light. One tap starts a session, and what opens is drawn from the sun’s own arithmetic rather than from an illustration. The curve is the real solar elevation at your latitude on today’s date. The shaded band under it is the stretch where there is enough UVB for synthesis to be plausible. The thicker overlay is the part of it you have spent outside. The disc is where the sun is right now.

Which means it looks flat and bandless in December, because December is — and at high latitude in midwinter it says the sun does not rise, rather than printing a nonsense time.

Alongside it run a stopwatch, live UV, the sun’s height and bearing, minutes outside, ambient dose, and an estimate labelled exactly as what it is: “estimated vitamin D: ~1,800–2,600 IU, research-model estimate · not a measurement.” It is personalised on the UV over your actual session, your latitude, the date, skin type, age, exposed body area, clothing, sunscreen and shade — and printed as a range, because the honest width of the estimate is wider than any single number implies.

The model plateaus rather than climbing. Past roughly one minimal erythemal dose your skin stops making more, and an app whose number keeps rising is quietly telling you that a longer burn is a bigger benefit.

And then the seam. Tap any finding anywhere — a coincidence on Insights, one half of an experiment, a lab period, a flare — and those exact days light up across the whole app. History reorders itself to show them, the temperature trace behind them marks them, the thirty-day sun history marks them too. One set of days, one banner naming what lit them, one Clear, surviving navigation. That is what makes it one connected memory rather than five separate features.

04

The problem being solved

A sample of one, with no control group, is extremely easy to lie to yourself with. You start a cream, you have a good fortnight, and the cream gets the credit. The hard problem in a personal health tracker is not collecting the data — it is refusing to over-read it.

So the app has one evidence ladder for everything it claims. Useful on an experiment and Useful on an insight are the same claim about the same kind of evidence: four rungs counted in paired days and in how far those days are spread. Forty days drawn from a single fortnight is capped at Emerging however clean it looks. Nothing is reported below the bar — Collecting is a progress bar and a count, not a hedged result, because a sentence with a caveat on it is still a sentence you will remember.

There are no confidence percentages anywhere in the app. A percentage is a promise about a population, and this is a sample of one. Why am I seeing this? opens the working instead: usable observations, days missing one side, the comparison window, how the days were split, consistency, the lag used, and the three limitations that are true of every finding this app can ever make.

Experiments split your days at your own median, so both halves exist, and grade on the smaller half — because fifty days with two above the line is two days of evidence wearing fifty days’ clothing. Underneath the result, every paired day is a dot placed by its own two values. Two clouds at different heights is the finding. Two overlapping clouds is the null result, drawn identically, because the app is not more excited about a positive.

05

Current capabilities

  • A check-in you designed. Question packs to start from, custom questions in your own words, and every question drawn with the control it will actually use — ten rungs, a yes beside a no, a keypad, three lines of text — with the honest cost printed above it: 14 questions, about 50 seconds a day.
  • The living timeline. Every day is a card; the day you just filled in flies into place as the first card and the rail draws downward into the days you have not lived yet.
  • Sun sessions with real solar geometry, first outdoor light after waking kept separately as a circadian number, the best upcoming window, and the next vitamin-D-producing one — which correctly says none in the next week in a British January instead of inventing one.
  • Personal experiments from a picker that can put anything against anything: a symptom against the pollen count, a meal against the next morning, a lab value against a season.
  • Labs & measurements that keep the reference range your laboratory printed. A result recorded without one gets no band, no colour and no verdict — this app does not know what normal is for somebody else’s assay. A new value arrives into its history with the delta counting up beside it and the band underneath filled in with what else the journal held during the gap.
  • Daily context, opt-in. Temperature, humidity, pressure and its change, UV, daylight, air quality and pollen. Coordinates are rounded to about a kilometre before anything is written or sent — the export column is called latitude_coarse because that is all there ever was.
  • Flares you mark yourself. Nothing is detected for you; a run of high scores is not always a flare, and you are the one who knows.
  • The appointment pack. Pick a window — including since my last appointment — and get a page that prints in the order a consultation actually runs.
  • Encrypted sync, opt-in. Encrypted on your device before it is uploaded, with a passphrase that never leaves it, derived at 600,000 PBKDF2-SHA256 iterations. Local saves never wait for the network.
  • Get your data out as CSV, multi-sheet Excel or JSON with date-range filters, plus a read-only backup viewer.
06

Design & technical decisions

Built for one thumb. Sheets rise from the bottom edge and stay welded to it, so their action row is the closest thing on screen to the thumb rather than the furthest. They are sized in dvh, because vh on iOS Safari means the viewport without the URL bar — which is exactly how a web form ends up hiding its own Save button. They get out of the keyboard’s way on both engines, through two different mechanisms feeding one CSS variable.

Months as rows, not weeks as columns. The year grid is drawn month-per-row for a plain reason: a phone gives a card about 330 px, which is 5 px per day laid out by week and 9 px laid out by day-of-month. Squares that small are not a tap target. So a tap names the day in a readout under the grid, next to the button that opens it — landing on the 14th when you meant the 15th is something you can see rather than a screen you have to back out of.

Colour is never the only channel. The grid is one tab stop with arrow-key movement, every square announces its date and score, and a month-by-month table underneath states the same figures in words for anyone who cannot use colour.

Soft Clinical. Soft graphite in the dark, warm off-white in the light, muted blue with sage, lavender and clay accents, and a deliberate hint of neobrutalism — borders a notch above a hairline, hard offset shadows, chunky buttons that press down into them. Calm, not loud. Backdrops are CSS: no WebGL, no canvas, no device test, nothing to fail.

The name is asked for, and refusable. A name and an age are a doorway, not step one of a registration — and the screen argues by consequence rather than by nagging: the greeting is quoted back with your own name in it as you type, and the header of an appointment pack fills in beside it. Age is stored as the year you were born, so it is still right in three years.

07

What changed across iterations

Twenty-one releases, taken from the project’s changelog. The direction is consistent: less to answer, more to trust.

1.15

Insights rebuilt around the questions people actually ask — the spread of days, flares, and the long view, instead of nine charts in a row.

1.16

The appointment pack. The realisation that a file for a spreadsheet and a page for a person are not the same errand, and the second one is the one that changes an appointment.

1.17

One tap is a whole day. The Daily Pulse; detail moved behind the rating rather than in front of it; navigation cut to two destinations and one verb.

1.18

How it’s drawn is your choice, and every choice says what it costs. Also: sheets stopped moving the page underneath them.

1.19

Quick Add is shaped like your condition. The dashboard assembles itself out of your own setup answers rather than filing them away to be discovered later.

1.20

The first run knows who it is for — and shows you the survey you are signing up for, printed exactly as it will be answered tomorrow morning.

1.21

Four things a journal cannot get from asking questions — weather context, sun sessions, personal experiments and lab results — and the one seam that makes them a single product rather than four features: tap a finding, and those exact days light up everywhere.

08

What comes next

  • More of the ladder, applied harder. The evidence ladder now governs experiments and insights; the weather-context observations are the next thing that should be held to exactly the same bar in the same module.
  • The iOS app to parity. The native build and Home Screen widget exist; the widget currently shows less than the dashboard knows.
  • Wearable import, widened. Google Fit and Fitbit takeout archives import today. The interesting version reads sleep staging and resting heart rate as first-class metrics rather than as numbers you typed.
  • A second opinion on the vitamin D model. It is a research-model estimate and labelled as one everywhere it appears, and it deserves review against the current literature by somebody who is not me.
