How I work

Five rules I did not
set out to have.

They came out of the work — an ambulance, a research methods course, a club roster and about a hundred thousand lines of trying things.

01

State the finding at its correct size

The most valuable thing Health Science Research Methods taught me was how to describe a result without inflating it. That is now a design constraint in software: Health Journal has no confidence percentages anywhere, because a percentage is a promise about a population and a journal is a sample of one. OWCS Comp Tracker returns UNKNOWN rather than a best guess. PaperAnimator cannot write a sentence the paper does not contain.

02

Design for the worst minute

Field medicine is a training in what happens when things are already going badly. It shows up in software as a preference for graceful degradation over impressive demos: Health Journal works with the network off; Manifester falls back to a device voice rather than going silent; SpellBomb’s privacy page is a file with no stylesheet, no script and no font, so it renders on a dead connection. This site is fully readable and navigable with JavaScript switched off.

03

Make the thing legible, then make it beautiful

Running a club is mostly reducing what people have to hold in their heads. The same instinct decides interfaces: what is the one number this screen is about, what does it cost the reader to find it, and what can be removed without losing anything. Aesthetics come after that, and then they matter enormously — a calm screen is not a decoration, it is the product working.

04

Write down the limits

Every repository behind this site has a section about what is not production-ready, what is not proven, and where a number came from. OWCS Comp Tracker’s honest-limits section is the most useful page in it. SpellBomb’s README corrects a claim an earlier version of itself made, in writing. That habit is not humility; it is how you stay able to trust your own system a year later.

05

Finish it

Anyone can start. The interesting part is the long tail — the reconnect path, the empty state, the keyboard route through a drag interaction, the export that has to open in real software, the test that fails when a content mistake would have reached a student. Six products got that far. That is the claim I would most want checked.

Taste

What I want a screen
to feel like.

Cinematic, futuristic and emotionally immersive — which in practice means far fewer elements than people expect, treated far more carefully.

Typography

Bold display, quiet body

A confident display face for the two or three things that matter, an exceptionally readable one underneath it, and crisp monospaced labels for anything technical. This page uses exactly three, plus a mono.

Motion

Meaning, not decoration

Movement should tell you something. On this site each project animates the way it behaves — SpellBomb burns a fuse, Health Journal grows, Manifester breathes, PaperAnimator unfolds. All of it stops completely under a reduced-motion preference.

Atmosphere

Near-black, warm ivory, one accent

Depth from restraint: a warm dark ground, a real paper surface for the document that is a document, and a single colour per page taken from the product it is about.

Sound

Only when asked

I build audio products and I do not autoplay audio. Manifester speaks when you press play; SpellBomb pronounces a word when a round starts. Nothing else on this site makes a noise.

Simplicity

Elegant, not empty

The difference is whether what was removed is still doing its job somewhere. Manifester keeps five things on the Create screen and everything else one row away — nothing was taken out, it was put in order.

Navigation

Never an experiment

Atmosphere is worth a lot and never worth an interaction somebody has to decode. The menu on this site is a plain element that opens with JavaScript switched off, and every link is a real link.

Outside the work

Sunlight, the gym,
the beach, the game.

Most of what is on this site started as something I wanted to understand properly rather than something I wanted to build.

Light, sleep and the everyday variables

Circadian rhythm, morning light, vitamin D, nutrition, training load. The unglamorous inputs with the strongest evidence behind them and the least attention paid to them. Health Journal’s sun sessions exist because I wanted to see the real solar elevation curve for my own latitude and date rather than an app’s illustration of one — and because I wanted an estimate that plateaus, since a number that keeps climbing is quietly telling you a longer burn is a bigger benefit.

Human performance

The gym is the most legible feedback loop I have access to: a variable you control, a measurement, and a delay before the answer. It is the same shape as every experiment in Health Journal, which is probably not a coincidence.

Competition

Overwatch, Deadlock and Marvel Rivals — and running the club around them. Competitive play is a fast education in decision-making with incomplete information, in reading a system while it is still moving, and in the fact that the best team is usually the one that communicates most clearly rather than the one with the best individual player.

Building things

Websites, apps and games. I like the ones that are genuinely hard — real time, real audio, real computer vision, real accessibility — and I like finishing them, which is the rarer half.

The beach

Camarillo is twenty minutes from the coast, and the answer to most difficult problems is a walk on it.
