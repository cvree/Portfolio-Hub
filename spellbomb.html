SpellBomb

The bomb is on you. Spell it, or it goes off.

One person hosts a room; everyone else joins from their own phone with a link or a four-letter code. A Node server owns the fuse, the hearts, the word and every outcome — the browsers only ever send intent. Two game modes share those rooms, that economy and that leaderboard, and the whole thing runs as a Discord Activity in a voice channel without becoming a second game.

01

What it is

A real-time party spelling game for a room full of people on their own devices. The host opens a room, everybody else joins with a four-letter code, and from then on one server keeps every bomb, every heart and every timer in step.

Two modes share the same rooms, the same players and everything around them:

  • Spell Bomb — a word, a fuse, and a bomb that gets passed. Play it as Passed Bomb, one player at a time, or Everyone Spells, where the whole room answers the same word in secret and it is revealed at once.
  • Word Rush — sixty seconds, one seven-letter honeycomb, everybody racing at the same time. Three rounds, cumulative scores, and a shared word wall at the end showing what everyone found and what the whole room walked past.
Runtime
Node 18+, Express, Socket.IO
Persistence

02

Why I built it

Running a campus esports club means running events, and the games that fill a room are almost never the competitive ones. They are the games where the person who has never played can win the first round. I wanted one that worked on whatever device somebody already had in their pocket, needed no install, no account and no explanation, and still had a moment in it worth shouting about.

The technical reason was the other half. A turn-based game with a timer is the smallest honest version of a real-time system: shared state, hostile latency, reconnects, and a strict requirement that everyone sees the same thing at the same time. I wanted to build one properly rather than read about it.

03

The most interesting interaction

The pass. When the bomb lands on you, three things happen in a specific order and each one is a decision.

First, the server guarantees you at least three seconds before it can possibly explode — MIN_TURN_MS. Nothing ruined an early match faster than receiving the bomb and having it go off in your hands, so a grace period is now a rule of the game rather than a hope about timing.

Second, the gap between turns is deliberately two different lengths. A pass is a beat: 700 ms, short enough that the bomb moves and the next word starts before the table notices the seam. A boom is a moment: 2,600 ms, because the client is putting the correct spelling on screen next to what you actually typed, and the room has to hold still long enough to read it. The integration test pins both ends of that range so neither drifts.

Third, the bomb always moves one seat clockwise — never at random. Seats are the room’s player order, which is the order the arena draws them in, so the path you see on screen is the path the server walked. Eliminated and disconnected seats are skipped. Only the very first holder of a match is chosen at random, and after that the game is legible: you can see it coming.

04

The problem being solved

Six people, six devices, six different networks, and one shared object that must be in exactly one place at any instant. The obvious failure is disagreement: two players each believing they hold the bomb, or a timer that ran differently on a slow phone.

The answer is that server.js is authoritative and the clients are not clever. Rounds, hearts, the fuse timer, word selection, grading, placements, gold, ladder points, inventory and every reward are decided server-side. A client sends intents — join, start, type a guess, ask for the word again — and renders whatever comes back. It cannot claim an outcome.

And there is one hole in it, which the README states plainly. The current word is sent to every client that is meant to hear it, because pronunciation happens in each browser through speechSynthesis, which takes text. A device that speaks the word necessarily has the word. In Passed Bomb that costs nothing — non-holders get a different word on their own turn. In Everyone Spells, where everyone is graded on that exact string, it is genuinely exploitable. So the competitive surface, the all-time ladder, is fed by Passed Bomb placement only, and is additionally gated behind a minimum match duration and turn count so it cannot be farmed by a scripted loop. The proper fix is rendering the dictation server-side and shipping audio instead of text. It is not done, and the documentation says it is not done.

05

Current capabilities

  • Two modes over one world. Passed Bomb, Everyone Spells and Word Rush share rooms, players, reconnects, spectators, cosmetics, chests and the leaderboard. There is no second economy.
  • Word Rush, in full. Seven letters with a required centre letter, a four-letter minimum, a pangram worth a flat +150, and a HEAT multiplier up to ×3 that decays on the clock but never on a wrong guess — so typing fast and missing costs you nothing but time.
  • 260 generated honeycombs built from SCOWL word lists unioned with the game’s own bank, each carrying its complete answer list, each clearing a stated quality bar. The generator is deterministic: the same inputs reproduce the same book.
  • A live typing readout. Every room update carries words-per-minute and accuracy per player, computed from the same per-match counters the achievements already keep. Both are null until there is enough evidence to state one, and the client draws a dash rather than inventing a zero.
  • Discord Activity. Start it in a voice channel and everybody in that channel lands in the same lobby with no name to type. It is the same client, the same protocol and the same rooms — a Discord lobby has an ordinary four-letter code, so a friend who is not on Discord at all can join it from the website.
  • Progression that cannot be forged. Chest tables, drop odds, prices, pity thresholds and anti-farm guards all live in one file, and the odds table the shop renders is literally the object the server rolls against. Prize cosmetics can only be earned, never bought or rolled.
  • Persistence that degrades honestly. With a database URL set, profiles live in Postgres; without one, they fall back to a JSON file; with a broken one, the server logs a warning and falls back rather than crashing.
  • No cookies at all. Not one — there is a test that checks. The privacy and terms pages are self-contained files with no stylesheet, script or font, so they render on a dead connection.
06

Design & technical decisions

The rules engine cannot reach a socket. Word Rush’s scoring, heat, announcer, awards and word wall live in a pure module with no network access at all. That is why “who won” is a unit test rather than an argument.

Discord support is plumbing, not a fork. Discord serves an Activity from its own domain and proxies requests through a /.proxy path prefix. Rather than teaching every route about it, the server strips that prefix off each incoming request before anything else sees it — done by wrapping the HTTP server’s emit, not with middleware, because Socket.IO matches its own path on the raw request including the WebSocket upgrade, which never reaches Express. No route, no cache rule and no test has to know Discord exists.

A public identifier is not a credential. A Discord user ID is public, so a Discord-backed profile is only ever handed to a socket that has presented an identity token this server signed. Guessing somebody’s snowflake gets you nothing.

A client backup restores cosmetics and nothing else. The browser keeps a snapshot so a player survives a server wipe, but gold, ladder points, wins and lifetime stats are never taken from it — an unsigned client blob is indistinguishable from a forged one.

Behind a load balancer, trust the right hop. The server caps concurrent sockets per client IP. Without proxy trust configured it reads the raw socket address, which behind a balancer is the balancer for every player alive — turning a per-player cap into a server-wide ceiling that silently disconnects player 41 with no message. The deployment blueprint sets that flag and explains why in a comment longer than the setting.

Update notes are written for players. “The bomb now moves in a circle”, not “refactored explodeBomb()”. Each release also gets a hand-drawn SVG in a deliberately crude MS-Paint idiom — no anti-aliasing, square caps, flat fills from the default sixteen-colour palette — and a test refuses any shipped version that does not have one.

07

What changed across iterations

Taken from the game’s own in-product update log, which is written for the person playing rather than the person reading the diff.

v8.0

Honest arrows, one-press sounds, a host who can steer. The point at which the arena stopped lying about where the bomb was going.

v8.1

The bomb goes round the table. Random passing replaced with one seat clockwise — the change that made the game readable.

v8.2

You can see what you’re typing. The letter tray, and the reveal card that shows the correct spelling beside what you actually wrote.

v8.5

Every word, reconsidered. A full pass over the word bank and its difficulty model.

v8.6

Your data, and the button that deletes it. Published privacy and terms pages, and a real delete path.

v8.7

A clock that actually moves. The fuse became the server’s clock rendered, rather than each browser’s guess at it.

v8.8

The arena finally uses the screen you gave it. Layout that adapts to the device instead of shrinking one design.

v9.0

SpellBomb is a Discord Activity now — the same client, the same rooms, one shared world.

v9.1

Word Rush — sixty seconds, seven letters, everybody at once. A second mode built entirely on the first one’s foundations.

v9.2

The shop gets a display case, and Word Rush stops arguing with you. Cosmetics you can actually see, and rules disputes resolved in the engine.

v9.3

The lobby answers back, and you can see how fast you spell. Live words-per-minute and accuracy, measured locally for responsiveness and authoritatively on the server.

08

What comes next

  • Close the Everyone Spells hole properly. Render the dictation server-side and ship audio bytes rather than text. Until that is done, that mode stays labelled as a party mode among people who trust each other, not a competitive one.
  • A stable public deployment. The Render blueprint and the Postgres path are both written and tested; the missing piece is a permanent host and the environment variables that go with it.
  • More of the room’s own voice. Word Rush’s announcer and awards are the most-liked part of a match, and the same treatment has not yet reached Passed Bomb.
