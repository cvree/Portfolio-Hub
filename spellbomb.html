SpellBomb

The bomb is on you. Spell it, or it goes off.

One person hosts; everyone else joins from their own phone with a link or a four-letter code. A Node server owns the fuse, the hearts, the word and every outcome — browsers only send intent. Two modes share those rooms, that economy and that leaderboard, and the whole thing runs as a Discord Activity without becoming a second game.

01

What it is

A real-time party spelling game for a room full of people on their own devices. The host opens a room, everybody joins with a four-letter code, and one server keeps every bomb, heart and timer in step.

Two modes, one set of rooms and players:

  • Spell Bomb — a word, a fuse, and a bomb that gets passed. Play it as Passed Bomb, one player at a time, or Everyone Spells, where the whole room answers the same word in secret and it is revealed at once.
  • Word Rush — sixty seconds, one seven-letter honeycomb, everybody at once. Three rounds, cumulative scores, and a word wall at the end showing what the room found and what it walked past.
Runtime
Node 18+, Express, Socket.IO
Persistence
Postgres, with a JSON-file fallback
Rush puzzles
260 pre-built honeycombs
Version
9.3.0
02

Why I built it

Running a campus esports club means running events, and the games that fill a room are never the competitive ones. They are the games where the person who has never played wins the first round. I wanted one that ran on whatever device was already in somebody’s pocket — no install, no account, no explanation — and still had a moment worth shouting about.

The other half was technical. A turn-based game with a timer is the smallest honest real-time system: shared state, hostile latency, reconnects, and everyone seeing the same thing at the same instant. I wanted to build one rather than read about one.

03

The most interesting interaction

The pass. When the bomb lands on you, three things happen in a specific order and each one is a decision.

First, the server guarantees three seconds before it can explode — MIN_TURN_MS. Nothing ruined an early match faster than catching the bomb and having it go off in your hands, so the grace period is a rule rather than a hope.

Second, the gap between turns has two lengths. A pass is a beat: 700 ms, short enough that the next word starts before the table notices the seam. A boom is a moment: 2,600 ms, long enough to read the correct spelling beside what you typed. An integration test pins both so neither drifts.

Third, the bomb moves one seat clockwise, never at random. Seats are the order the arena draws them in, so the path on screen is the path the server walked; eliminated and disconnected seats are skipped. Only the first holder is random. After that you can see it coming.

04

The problem being solved

Six people, six devices, six networks, one shared object that must be in exactly one place at any instant. The failure is disagreement: two players both holding the bomb, or a timer that ran differently on a slow phone.

So server.js is authoritative and the clients are not clever. Rounds, hearts, the fuse, word selection, grading, placements, gold, ladder points and every reward are decided server-side. A client sends intent and renders what comes back. It cannot claim an outcome.

One hole in it, which the README states plainly. Pronunciation happens in each browser through speechSynthesis, which takes text — so a device that speaks the word has the word. In Passed Bomb that costs nothing: non-holders get a different word on their turn. In Everyone Spells, where everyone is graded on that exact string, it is exploitable. The all-time ladder is therefore fed by Passed Bomb placement only, gated behind a minimum match duration and turn count so a scripted loop cannot farm it. The proper fix is server-side dictation shipped as audio. It is not done, and the documentation says so.

05

Current capabilities

  • Two modes over one world. Passed Bomb, Everyone Spells and Word Rush share rooms, players, reconnects, spectators, cosmetics, chests and the leaderboard. There is no second economy.
  • Word Rush, in full. Seven letters with a required centre, a four-letter minimum, a flat +150 pangram, and a HEAT multiplier to ×3 that decays on the clock but never on a wrong guess — missing costs nothing but time.
  • 260 generated honeycombs from SCOWL word lists unioned with the game’s own bank, each carrying its complete answer list and clearing a stated quality bar. The generator is deterministic: same inputs, same book.
  • A live typing readout. Every room update carries words-per-minute and accuracy per player, from the counters the achievements already keep. Both stay null until there is evidence for a number, and the client draws a dash rather than inventing a zero.
  • Discord Activity. Start it in a voice channel and everybody lands in one lobby with no name to type. Same client, same protocol, same rooms — the lobby still has a four-letter code, so a friend who is not on Discord can join from the website.
  • Progression that cannot be forged. Chest tables, drop odds, prices, pity thresholds and anti-farm guards live in one file, and the odds table the shop renders is the object the server rolls against. Prize cosmetics are earned only.
  • Persistence that degrades honestly. With a database URL set, profiles live in Postgres; without one, they fall back to a JSON file; with a broken one, the server logs a warning and falls back rather than crashing.
  • No cookies at all. Not one, and a test checks. The privacy and terms pages carry no stylesheet, script or font, so they render on a dead connection.
06

Design & technical decisions

The rules engine cannot reach a socket. Word Rush’s scoring, heat, announcer, awards and word wall live in a pure module with no network access at all. That is why “who won” is a unit test rather than an argument.

Discord support is plumbing, not a fork. Discord proxies an Activity through a /.proxy path prefix. Rather than teach every route about it, the server strips the prefix off each request first — by wrapping the HTTP server’s emit, not with middleware, because Socket.IO matches its own path on the raw request including the WebSocket upgrade, which never reaches Express. No route, cache rule or test has to know Discord exists.

A public identifier is not a credential. A Discord user ID is public, so a Discord-backed profile only goes to a socket holding an identity token this server signed. Guessing a snowflake gets you nothing.

A client backup restores cosmetics and nothing else. The browser keeps a snapshot so a player survives a server wipe, but gold, ladder points, wins and lifetime stats never come from it — an unsigned client blob is indistinguishable from a forged one.

Behind a load balancer, trust the right hop. The server caps concurrent sockets per client IP. Without proxy trust it reads the raw socket address, which behind a balancer is the balancer for every player alive — a per-player cap becomes a server-wide ceiling that silently drops player 41. The deployment blueprint sets that flag, and explains why in a comment longer than the setting.

Update notes are written for players. “The bomb now moves in a circle”, not “refactored explodeBomb()”. Each release also gets a hand-drawn SVG in a crude MS-Paint idiom — no anti-aliasing, square caps, flat sixteen-colour fills — and a test refuses any version without one.

07

What changed across iterations

From the game’s own update log, written for the person playing rather than the person reading the diff.

v8.0

Honest arrows, one-press sounds, a host who can steer. The point at which the arena stopped lying about where the bomb was going.

v8.1

The bomb goes round the table. Random passing replaced with one seat clockwise — the change that made the game readable.

v8.2

You can see what you’re typing. The letter tray, and the reveal card that shows the correct spelling beside what you actually wrote.

v8.5

Every word, reconsidered. A full pass over the word bank and its difficulty model.

v8.6

Your data, and the button that deletes it. Published privacy and terms pages, and a real delete path.

v8.7

A clock that actually moves. The fuse became the server’s clock rendered, rather than each browser’s guess at it.

v8.8

The arena finally uses the screen you gave it. Layout that adapts to the device instead of shrinking one design.

v9.0

SpellBomb is a Discord Activity now — the same client, the same rooms, one shared world.

v9.1

Word Rush — sixty seconds, seven letters, everybody at once. A second mode built entirely on the first one’s foundations.

v9.2

The shop gets a display case, and Word Rush stops arguing with you. Cosmetics you can actually see, and rules disputes resolved in the engine.

v9.3

The lobby answers back, and you can see how fast you spell. Live words-per-minute and accuracy, measured locally for responsiveness and authoritatively on the server.

08

What comes next

  • Close the Everyone Spells hole properly. Render dictation server-side and ship audio, not text. Until then the mode stays labelled a party mode among people who trust each other.
  • A stable public deployment. The Render blueprint and the Postgres path are written and tested; what is missing is a permanent host.
  • More of the room’s own voice. Word Rush’s announcer and awards are the most-liked part of a match, and Passed Bomb has not had that treatment yet.
