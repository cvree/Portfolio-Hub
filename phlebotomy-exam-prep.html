Phlebotomy
Exam Prep

Know what to study before the exam, not after it.

A focused certification platform built around one loop: learn, practise, get feedback, find the weak area, drill it, test, improve. Every session should leave a student knowing what they know, what they are weak at, and what to do next — not just a percentage. I built it while doing the training myself.

01

What it is

A study platform for phlebotomy students preparing for certification. The first certification it targets is the NHA Certified Phlebotomy Technician, but certification configuration is data rather than structure, so ASCP PBT and NCCT can be added without rebuilding the application.

It is also built for California candidates specifically. Alongside the national exam material there is a dedicated study area covering the CDPH Laboratory Field Services CPT1/CPT2 state licensing pathway that California layers on top of national certification — the part that is hardest to find written down anywhere.

02

Why I built it

I am in phlebotomy training. I completed the initial coursework in July 2026 and I am preparing for the NHA CPT exam. Every study resource I could find did the same thing: gave me a score, and left the actual work — what do I do about that score? — entirely to me.

A score is not a study plan. Seventy-two percent tells you almost nothing: it does not say whether the misses were clustered in one domain, whether they were recall failures or reasoning failures, or whether the eight you got right were luck. So the product promise here is deliberately not “pass your exam” — it is know what to study. That is a promise the software can actually keep.

Building it while learning the material had a side effect I did not expect. Writing 513 questions and 181 definitions that survive their own test suite is, itself, the most thorough way to study the material I have found.

03

The most interesting interaction

The Order of Draw drill. Order of draw is the piece of phlebotomy knowledge that has to be automatic, because getting it wrong contaminates a specimen with additive carried over from the previous tube and nobody downstream can tell. Every student learns a mnemonic. Almost nobody learns the reason.

So the drill has four modes, and they test four different things:

  • Arrange the sequence — put all six CLSI collection positions in order from scratch.
  • What next — continue a draw already under way, which is the version the chairside actually asks.
  • Spot the error — find the one tube out of place in an otherwise correct sequence.
  • Fill the gaps — complete a part-built sequence.

Every answer names what was misplaced and explains why each position sits where it does. The side panel is explicit about the mnemonic being scaffolding: “Once the sequence is automatic, work on the reason behind it — exam questions are more often about carryover than about reciting colors.” And beside it, the thing nobody tells students until they are on a floor: read the label, not the cap. Tube colours are a convention that varies by manufacturer.

04

The problem being solved

Exam prep fails students in two specific ways, and the app is designed against both.

It reports the wrong number. A percentage is a summary of the past. What a student needs is a decision about the next hour. So the results screen leads with the weak-area insight rather than the score; mastery per area runs from Not started to Strong, weighted toward recent answers and gated on exposure; and the recommendations are deterministic and explainable — the app can always say why it is suggesting what it is suggesting, in plain language.

It re-serves the same questions. A mock exam you have already sat is a memory test, not a mock exam. So every paper prefers questions you have not met on your last three exams — and freshness never shortens a paper: once an area’s unseen questions run out, seen ones fill it. The bank is deep enough that three consecutive full-length papers share no question at all, and there is a test that fails the build if any area ever becomes too shallow for that to hold.

The readiness score is careful about what it is not. It is a measure of how much of the material you have demonstrated recently. The screen says so out loud: “It is not a prediction of whether you will pass, and we make no claim that it is.”

05

Current capabilities

  • 513 original certification-style questions across ten study areas, with immediate inline feedback: the verdict, the correct answer, a full explanation, why the specific distractor you picked was wrong, and a memory tip where one helps.
  • Six session modes — Quick 10, all areas, a single area, weak areas, missed questions, and unseen questions. Sessions autosave and resume after a refresh.
  • Order of Draw, four modes over the six CLSI collection positions, with three interchangeable interactions and full keyboard operation.
  • Tube & additive mastery in four directions — tube to additive, additive to tube, tube to specimen type, tube to common use — explaining the mechanism after every answer. Distractors that are as true as the answer are rejected, so no question has two defensible options.
  • 181 vocabulary terms, each carrying what it is, what it is part of (EDTA belongs to the lavender tube; the median cubital vein belongs to the antecubital fossa), the word parts it is built from, why it matters at the chairside, a mnemonic where one helps, and the terms it is genuinely confused with.
  • Five study modes per set — flashcards, adaptive Learn that promotes recognition to typed recall, Write with near-miss spelling graded “close” rather than wrong, timed Match, and a no-feedback Test — over thirteen sets, with spaced repetition scheduling every term individually.
  • Three timed mock papers with a wall-clock timer that survives a sleeping phone or a closed tab, a question grid with answered and flagged state, submit confirmation, and a domain breakdown afterwards.
  • Long-form study guides on order of draw, tube colours and additives, specimen handling, venipuncture complications and California CPT1/CPT2 licensing — each linking directly into the matching practice or drill.
  • No account. Progress lives in the browser, exports to JSON, and can be reset.
06

Design & technical decisions

Warm ivory, not clinical white. Study happens in long sittings, often at night. The surface is a warm off-white with a faint grid, the accent is a deep medical teal, and the question type is set in a serif at a size you can read across a desk. The tube illustrations are drawn in their real cap colours because that is the thing being learned.

Three interactions for one action. The arrange drill supports a pointer drag, a two-tap swap and per-row buttons — not because drag is unreliable, but because on a phone in a break room, with one hand, a drag is the wrong instrument. Every one of them is keyboard-operable, and each row announces its own position and what moving it would do.

Content mistakes fail CI. 257 tests cover the places where a bug would quietly mislead a student: scoring, question selection, mock-paper construction and freshness, mastery and readiness calculation, weak-area detection, recommendation rules, storage migrations, streak arithmetic, order-of-draw grading and tube-drill generation. The suite also asserts structural integrity of the question bank itself — so a content mistake fails the build rather than reaching a student.

No server, on purpose. Progress is a student’s own business, and a study app that needs an account is a study app that has a breach risk and a login screen between the student and the material. It builds to a static export and hosts anywhere.

Search covers what people type. The glossary search matches the term, its abbreviations and its definition, so “purple tube” finds EDTA.

07

What changed across iterations

First

A question bank and a score. The version that proved the score was not the useful part.

Then

Explanations that name the distractor. Telling a student the right answer teaches them one fact; telling them why the option they chose was wrong teaches them the distinction the exam is testing.

Then

Mastery, readiness and deterministic recommendations — the point where results stopped being a report and started being a next step, with the reasoning shown in plain language.

Then

Drills split out from practice. Order of draw and tube mastery are procedural, not propositional; multiple choice was the wrong instrument for both.

Then

Vocabulary became its own product — thirteen sets, five study modes and spaced repetition — after it became obvious that most wrong answers were vocabulary failures wearing a clinical question’s clothes.

Then

California CPT1/CPT2. The national exam is only half of what a California candidate has to navigate, and the state pathway is genuinely hard to find written plainly.

Latest

The bank expanded to 513 questions and three mock forms — deep enough that three consecutive full-length papers share no question, with a test that enforces it.

08

What comes next

  • Sit the exam. The most useful next iteration is the one written after I have taken the NHA CPT myself and know which of my assumptions about it were wrong.
  • Sources and caveats on every content item. The app currently marks its content status as awaiting sources and review, and says so on screen rather than hiding it. Citing each item to CLSI, NHA and CDPH material is the next substantial piece of work.
  • A second certification. ASCP PBT is configuration rather than architecture, which was the point of building it that way — but its blueprint differs enough that the area weightings need real research, not a copy.
  • Clinical-scenario questions. The bank is strong on recall and application; the hardest exam items are short scenarios with a judgement call in them, and those are the slowest to write well.
